\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}

Every setting of the guide except scope and conversation state is supported on at least one of the three
public datasets, whose human grades are independent of the authors. PT-CS is the only source of complete
exams, so grading scope and the conversation sub-study rest
on it alone; none of the public datasets links answers into exams. The PT-CS exams are short, so the scope
result holds for exams of a few questions. The PT-CS grades are a joint teacher decision with no
per-teacher record, so no agreement ceiling between teachers can be computed on PT-CS.

The results are conditional on the served backends, whose quantisation, inference software, and hardware
differ across providers, so what carries over to another backend is the comparison between
configurations rather than the absolute scores; temperature 0 does not make grading deterministic either.
Reasoning token counts come from each provider's own accounting, so they are best
compared within a model. The follow-up experiment on the grading instruction was designed after the zero-collapse had been
found, so its wordings were chosen with that failure in mind; the wordings and the rule for adopting one
were fixed before they were run, and other wordings may do better or worse.

The guide was built and validated on the five models and the datasets of this study, so its standing in
the two validation tests may differ for other models and data. Across
all experiments, code evidence is narrower than
short-answer evidence, resting on two Java-centric datasets, so we draw the code conclusions more
cautiously. The guidance contrast bundles two different interventions, a rubric (PT-CS, RIAYN) and a
reference answer (Mohler, SemEval), which we report separately rather than as one combined effect. The
expensive factors were varied one at a time rather than fully crossed, so interactions between them, such
as reasoning with decomposition, are unmeasured.
