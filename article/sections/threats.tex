\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}

The guide's rules rest mainly on the three public datasets, whose human grades are independent of the
authors. PT-CS is the only source of complete exams, so grading scope and the conversation sub-study rest
on it alone; none of the public datasets links answers into exams. The PT-CS exams are short, so the scope
result holds for exams of a few questions. Three further properties of PT-CS bound what it can show. Its
answers are those whose suggested grade the teachers changed, and such answers may differ in difficulty
from typical ones, so absolute PT-CS levels need not represent all the answers the platform graded. The
grades are a joint teacher decision with no per-teacher record, so no agreement ceiling between teachers
can be computed on PT-CS. And because the platform's suggestions came from an AI model, agreement could be
inflated if that model resembled one we test; keeping only answers whose grade the teachers changed limits
this influence.

The results are conditional on the served backends, whose quantisation, inference software, and hardware
differ across providers, so what carries over to another backend is the comparison between
configurations rather than the absolute scores; temperature 0 does not make grading deterministic either
(Section~\ref{sec:rq1}). Reasoning token counts come from each provider's own accounting, so they are best
compared within a model. The grading-instruction experiment was designed after the zero-collapse had been
found, so its wordings were chosen with that failure in mind; the wordings and the rule for adopting one
were fixed before they were run, and other wordings may do better or worse.

The guide was built on the model families tested. Across all experiments, code evidence is narrower than
short-answer evidence, resting on two Java-centric datasets, so we draw the code conclusions more
cautiously. The guidance contrast bundles two different interventions, a rubric (PT-CS, RIAYN) and a
reference answer (Mohler, SemEval), which we report separately rather than as one combined effect. The
expensive factors were varied one at a time rather than fully crossed, so interactions between them, such
as reasoning with decomposition, are unmeasured.
