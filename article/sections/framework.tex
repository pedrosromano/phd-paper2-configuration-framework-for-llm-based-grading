An instructor who sets up an LLM grader must settle every configuration choice at once, while each
comparison in the results changed one choice and kept the others fixed. The configuration guide combines
those comparisons into a single configuration that can be used with any model, so that the instructor
does not have to weigh the evidence choice by choice. Every choice was judged on the three measures used
throughout: agreement with the teachers (QWK), consistency across repeated gradings, and cost per grade.
One rule decided each setting: keep the option that raised agreement reliably, and where no option did,
keep the cheaper and more consistent one.

\subsection{The Guide}
\label{sec:guide}

The guide has six settings (Table~\ref{tab:framework}), and Fig.~\ref{fig:frameworkdiagram} shows it at
work on one exam. Two settings raise agreement: give the model the rubric or the reference answer, and ask
it to award partial credit. The other four keep grading cheap, consistent, and fair: grade with reasoning
off, one question per call, with a single holistic score, in a clean conversation for each answer. The
guide sets how the grader is configured and leaves the choice of model open, so we validated it on all
five models of the study.

\subsection{How the Guide Was Validated}
\label{sec:guidetests}

We validated the guide with two tests that compare it with every configuration graded in the study, so
that the guide faces every alternative we ran.

\emph{Test 1: the guide against the other configurations of the same model.} For each model and
dataset, the guide's configuration is compared with each other configuration of the same model, on the
answers both graded. The alternatives are no guidance, the strict instruction, partial credit with a
justification written first, the whole exam in one call, a score per criterion, one conversation per exam,
and reasoning on. The alternatives other than the instruction ones were graded with the strict
instruction, so they also differ from the guide in that setting. Each comparison uses the same bootstrap
as the results, without correction for multiple comparisons, which makes an advantage of the alternative
easier to detect. The alternative is then counted as significantly better than the guide, not
significantly different, or significantly worse.

\emph{Test 2: the guide against the best configuration of any model, and at what cost.} For each
dataset, every configuration graded without reasoning, of any model, is placed by its agreement with the
teachers and its cost per 1000 grades, on the answers that all of them graded. The test asks how close the
guide comes to the best configuration in the study, with each model, and whether any cheaper
configuration agrees better. The configurations that no cheaper one beats form the cost-quality frontier.
The same comparison on the sampled answers adds the configurations with reasoning on.

\subsection{Validation Results}
\label{sec:guideuse}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\input{figures/fig_guide_test1.pgf}
\caption{The guide against the other configurations of the same model (Test 1). (a) For each alternative,
the number of model-dataset comparisons in which it agreed with the teachers significantly better than the
guide, not significantly differently, or significantly worse (95\% bootstrap intervals, without
correction). (b) The change in agreement in each comparison, alternative minus guide, coloured by model;
filled points are significant, and the shaded band is the $\pm0.05$ equivalence margin. (c) The
alternative's cost per grade relative to the guide's. The alternatives other than the grading
instruction used the strict instruction.}
\label{fig:guidetest1}
\end{figure*}

Of the 56 comparisons without reasoning, the guide agreed significantly better with the teachers than
the alternative in 33, showed no significant difference in 22, and agreed worse in one: Claude Sonnet 4.6
with the strict instruction on the 84 PT-CS short answers, by 0.043 QWK (Fig.~\ref{fig:guidetest1}). No
alternative to the guidance, the scope, the decomposition, or the clean conversation agreed better than
the guide for any model. Most alternatives also made grades less consistent (34 of the 56 comparisons),
and every alternative except dropping the guidance or the partial-credit request cost more per grade.

Reasoning is the one setting in which the guide gives something up. With reasoning on, agreement rose
significantly in 7 of 17 comparisons, five of them for Qwen3.5, the model with the strongest
zero-collapse, and never fell; but grades became less consistent in 14 of the 17, and each grade cost
2.6 to 179 times as much. The guide keeps reasoning off for that cost and that loss of consistency.

Across models, the guide's configuration reached or approached the best agreement in the study
(Fig.~\ref{fig:guidevalidation}). With the model best suited to each dataset, it was the best
configuration on Mohler (GPT-5.1, 0.653 QWK) and on PT-CS code (DeepSeek-V4-Flash, 0.674), and came within
0.002 of the best on SemEval, 0.014 on RIAYN, and 0.043 on the PT-CS short answers. On the sampled answers,
which add the configurations with reasoning, the best configuration led it by at most 0.053. With four of
the five models it graded code at 0.65 to 0.76 QWK, and with Qwen3.5 at 0.50 and 0.55, as that model's
zero-collapse persisted under partial credit. With the cheapest model, DeepSeek-V4-Flash, it cost 0.02 to
0.09 euros per thousand grades, and no cheaper configuration agreed better, apart from a difference of
0.001 QWK on SemEval.

Overall, the guide held its ground in both tests: within each model, almost no alternative without
reasoning agreed better with the teachers, and across models it came within 0.05 QWK of the best
configuration on every dataset.
