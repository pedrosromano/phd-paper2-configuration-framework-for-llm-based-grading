An instructor who sets up an LLM grader must settle every configuration choice at once. The configuration guide
we present is a single configuration for any model, built from the best settings found in the previous sections and
judged on the three measures used throughout: agreement with the human graders
(QWK), consistency across repeated gradings, and cost per grade. One rule decided each setting: keep the option
that raised agreement, and where the options agreed equally well, the cheaper and more consistent one. The guide fixes six of
the seven configuration choices and leaves the seventh, the model, open. It was then tested on the data it was built from and validated on two datasets that
played no part in building it.

\subsection{The Guide}
\label{sec:guide}


The guide takes the settings the results point to (Table~\ref{tab:framework}), and
Fig.~\ref{fig:frameworkdiagram} shows it at work on one exam. Two settings raise agreement: give the model the
rubric or the reference answer, and ask for partial credit, with the code prompt when the answer is code and the
partial-credit prompt otherwise. The other four keep grading cheap, consistent, and fair: reasoning off, one
question per call, a single holistic score, and a clean conversation for each answer. Reasoning stays
off on every task: its largest gains came from repairing the zero-collapse on code, which the code prompt does at
a small fraction of the cost. The guide leaves the choice of model open, so we tested it on all five models. It
returns a score and, on code, a statement of at most two sentences on what earned marks; fuller feedback for
students needs a separate request. Fig.~\ref{fig:guideprompt} gives the guide's prompt as used in the
experiments.

\subsection{How the Guide Was Tested}
\label{sec:guidetests}

%% Pedro's review of 2026-11-06 (option A): the guide is judged model by model against the baseline and against
%% reasoning on, in agreement, consistency and cost, the same way on the study's data and on the validation datasets;
%% the former Tests 1 and 2 are in the supplementary material. Numbers: guide_eval.references / references_summary
%% (tests: within_model; validation: verification.contrasts). Keep this simple (Pedro: "o guia e simples e os seus
%% testes tambem").
The guide leaves the model open, so it is judged model by model against two references from the results. The
first is the baseline configuration, the starting point of every comparison, which differs from the guide only in
its prompt: it grades strictly. The second is the same baseline with reasoning on, the most expensive setting. To be
considered good, the guide should agree better than the baseline and almost as well as reasoning, at a much lower cost. All three
configurations come from runs already reported, so nothing was graded again. Each comparison uses the answers both
configurations graded, which with reasoning on are the sampled answers, and the bootstrap of the results, without
correction for multiple comparisons, so that any advantage over the guide is easier to detect. The tests make these
comparisons on the four datasets the guide was built from, and the validation repeats them on the two validation
datasets.

\subsection{Test Results}
\label{sec:guideuse}

The guide never agreed worse than the baseline (Fig.~\ref{fig:guiderefs}a). It agreed significantly better in 10 of
the 22 comparisons: in 7 of the 10 on code, where the zero-collapse appeared, and in 3 of the 12 on
short answers, there by at most 0.030 QWK. It cost 1.05 to 1.85 times as much per grade as the baseline, for the
longer prompt and, on code, the short statement before the score. On code its grades also varied a little more than
the baseline's (Fig.~\ref{fig:guiderefs}b), because partial credit spreads grades that the strict prompt had pushed
to zero.

The guide, which always has reasoning off, was almost as good as reasoning. Averaged over the 17 comparisons, the
two agreed equally well: the difference was 0.002 QWK, in the guide's favour. Reasoning on agreed significantly
better in four, three of them for Qwen3.5, and significantly worse in two, both
for DeepSeek-V4-Flash on code; on code it beat the guide only once, for Qwen3.5 on RIAYN. It made grades less
consistent than the guide in 13 of the 17 comparisons, including all nine on short answers, and cost 2.6 to 179
times as much per grade (Fig.~\ref{fig:guiderefs}c).

Overall, on the data it was built from, the guide improved on the baseline without ever doing worse, and it was
almost as good as reasoning, with more consistent grades and at a small fraction of the cost. The supplementary
material compares the guide in the same way with every other configuration graded without reasoning: within each
model it agreed significantly better in 40 of 70 comparisons and never worse, and with the model best suited to each
dataset it was the best of them on three of the five datasets.

\subsection{Guide Validation}
\label{sec:validation}

%% Numbers: experiments/analysis/verification.py (protocol and criterion fixed before any call, CLAUDE.md S11) through
%% guide_eval.references. Pedro (2026-11-06): HARMOGEN-R and SAF appear only here, as validation; no development step
%% of the code prompt is reported on them.
A guide built and tested on the same answers may fit those answers alone, so the validation repeats the comparison
on 300 SAF short answers and 129 HARMOGEN-R code answers that played no part in building it (the lower part of
Fig.~\ref{fig:guiderefs}). There, reasoning on was graded with the partial-credit prompt instead of the strict one.

The validation results followed those of the tests. The guide agreed significantly better than the baseline in 6
of the 10 comparisons, for four of the five models on code, by 0.046 to 0.146 QWK, and for two on short answers,
and it never agreed worse. Under the guide, the five
models agreed with the human grades at 0.62 to 0.72 QWK on SAF and 0.72 to 0.91 on HARMOGEN-R. With GLM-5.1 it
agreed with the instructors' mean as closely as each instructor agrees with the mean of the other three (0.91
against 0.90 to 0.93), and four of the five models gave zero to 5 to 10\% of the code answers, close to the
instructors' 7\%.

Reasoning on agreed significantly better than the guide in 4 of the 8 comparisons: twice for Qwen3.5, by 0.092 and
0.197 QWK, and once each for DeepSeek-V4-Flash and GPT-5.1, by 0.092 and 0.095. It never agreed worse and made grades
less consistent in 5 of the 8 comparisons. Its main drawback was the cost: 3.2 to 55 times as much per grade as the
guide.

The criterion fixed in advance asked the guide to agree at least as well as every alternative without reasoning,
with every model; besides the baseline, these alternatives were no guidance and, on code, a score per criterion and
the partial-credit prompt. It was met on SAF with every model and on HARMOGEN-R with three of the five: a score per
criterion agreed better for GLM-5.1, by 0.025, and the partial-credit prompt for GPT-5.1, by 0.038 (supplementary
material).

Overall, the guide held up on data it was not built from. It never did worse than the baseline, and with every model
but Qwen3.5 it came within 0.1 QWK of reasoning, with no significant difference in four of those six comparisons.
The guide thus kept almost all the agreement that reasoning brought, without its cost.
