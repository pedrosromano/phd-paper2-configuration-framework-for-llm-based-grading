\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusion}

An instructor adopting an LLM grader faces several choices at once, and prior studies addressed them only
in fragments. We tested them together for computer-science grading under one controlled protocol and
condensed the result into one recipe. The central finding is that the cheap changes were the effective
ones. Supplying a rubric or a reference answer raised agreement significantly in thirteen of fifteen
model-dataset combinations, by 0.10 to 0.34 QWK, for 1.2 to 1.8 times the cost per grade, and asking the
model to award partial credit raised code agreement by up to 0.11 QWK for a few extra input tokens. Reasoning raised
agreement significantly in six of twenty combinations, three after correcting for multiple comparisons, while
multiplying output tokens by up to 925 and usually making grades less consistent. Its clearest gains came on
code, for models that scored many partially correct answers as zero. This zero-collapse is a property of the
model with reasoning off: asking for partial credit reduced it in GPT-5.1, but not in Qwen3.5, which names
what the student did right and still gives no marks.
Grading a whole exam in one call never helped, scoring criterion by criterion helped only GPT-5.1 on one
dataset, both saved little or nothing, and the closed models did not repay their price on code.

The recipe follows directly: the rubric or reference answer, an instruction to award partial credit, one
question per call with reasoning off and a single holistic score, a clean conversation per answer, and
DeepSeek-V4-Flash, the cheapest model we tested, which with partial credit graded code as well as the
strongest model without reasoning at a tenth of its cost. Applied to every dataset, the recipe was the best
configuration without reasoning on PT-CS code and the second on RIAYN, and stayed within 0.05 QWK of the
best on the public short-answer datasets.

Future work follows from the gaps this study declares and from what the literature has yet to settle.
Few-shot guidance, adding a few already-graded answers to the prompt as examples, was held out; the strong
effect of the studied guidance makes it worth testing, but only with a principled rule for choosing the
examples. A fine-tuning arm also remains open: fine-tuned models can match or exceed earlier supervised
graders~\cite{cong_memorization_2026}, but fine-tuning has not yet been placed in a guide of this kind,
comparing a fine-tuned grader against its base model on agreement, consistency, and cost together. Code
evidence rests on two Java-centric datasets because code corpora graded with human rubrics are
scarce~\cite{havare_ai-based_2025,pathak_rubric_2025}, so widening it to other languages and paradigms, and
contributing such data, is the clearest way to firm up the code conclusions. PT-CS comes from one
institution, so testing the recipe across more institutions and student populations would establish how
far it carries. And the zero-collapse, where a grader scores many partially correct answers as zero, is not,
to our knowledge, described in the grading literature; it deserves study in its own right, both to detect it
and to prevent it without paying for reasoning.

For the instructor the introduction began with, the answer is a short routine: supply the rubric or
reference answer, ask for partial credit, and grade one question per call with reasoning off on
DeepSeek-V4-Flash, for under 0.10 euros per thousand grades.
