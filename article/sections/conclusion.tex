\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusion}

An instructor adopting an LLM grader faces several choices at once, and prior studies addressed them only
in fragments. We tested them together for computer-science grading under one controlled protocol. The
central finding is that the cheap changes were the effective ones. Supplying a rubric or a reference answer raised agreement significantly in thirteen of fifteen
model-dataset combinations, by 0.095 to 0.341 QWK, for 1.2 to 1.8 times the cost per grade, and asking the
model to award partial credit raised code agreement by up to 0.11 QWK for a few extra input tokens. Reasoning raised
agreement significantly in five of twenty combinations, three after correcting for multiple comparisons, while
multiplying output tokens by up to 925 and usually making grades less consistent. Its clearest gains came on
code, for models that scored many partially correct answers as zero. This zero-collapse is a property of the
model with reasoning off: asking for partial credit reduced it in GPT-5.1, but not in Qwen3.5, which names
what the student did right and still gives no marks.
Grading a whole exam in one call never helped, scoring criterion by criterion helped only GPT-5.1 on one
dataset, both saved little or nothing, and on code the closed models bought little for their price.

Putting together the options that worked best in each comparison, we built a configuration guide that
leaves the choice of model open: give the model the rubric or reference answer and ask it for partial
credit, and grade one question per call, with reasoning off, a single holistic score, and a clean
conversation for each answer. Tested on five models against the 56 other configurations of the same
models without reasoning, the guide agreed significantly better with the teachers in 59\% of the
comparisons and showed no significant difference in the other 41\%; none agreed significantly better. With the model best suited
to each dataset, it matched or came within 0.05 QWK of the best configuration in the study, and with the
cheapest model no cheaper configuration graded better, apart from a difference of 0.001 QWK on one
dataset. Validated on two datasets that played no part in building it, the guide met the criterion fixed
in advance on short answers with every model. On code it met it with four of the five models: with GLM-5.1
and GPT-5.1 it graded nearly as well as the instructors agree with each other, while Qwen3.5 and
DeepSeek-V4-Flash showed the zero-collapse under it.

Three directions for future work follow. The zero-collapse, a specific form of the severity that earlier
work measured as leniency, deserves study in its own right, to detect it and to prevent it without paying
for reasoning. The code evidence rests on three Java and C++ datasets, two of them from single courses, so
the guide should be tested on other programming languages, institutions, and student populations. Two options were left
out of the guide, few-shot examples of already-graded answers and fine-tuning, and each should be weighed
against it on agreement, consistency, and cost.

For the instructor the introduction began with, the answer is a short routine: supply the rubric or
reference answer, ask for partial credit, and grade one question per call with reasoning off, in a clean
conversation; with an inexpensive model this costs under 0.10 euros per thousand grades, and on code
the model must be one that does not collapse to zeros.
