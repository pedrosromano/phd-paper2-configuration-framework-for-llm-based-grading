\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusion}

An instructor adopting an LLM grader faces several choices at once, and prior studies addressed them
only in fragments. We tested them together for computer-science grading under one controlled protocol
and condensed the result into a configuration guide. The central finding is that the expensive settings
rarely paid and the cheapest change almost always did. Reasoning raised agreement significantly in five of
twenty model-dataset combinations, two after correcting for multiple comparisons, while multiplying
output tokens by up to 925 and usually making grades less consistent; its clearest gains came on code,
for models that scored many partially correct answers as zero, the zero-collapse, which a zero-rate
check on teacher-graded answers detects. Evaluation guidance, for 1.2 to 1.8 times the cost per grade,
raised agreement significantly in thirteen of fifteen combinations across three model
families. Grading a whole exam in one call and scoring criterion by criterion brought no significant
gain and saved little or nothing, and the closed frontier model did not repay its price on the public data. In a real
deployment these effects held, while the fine ranking of close configurations did not, which is why the
guide confirms that choice locally. The guide's default follows directly: the strongest model that does not collapse to zero, with
reasoning off, guidance, one question per call, holistic scoring, and a clean conversation. It stayed within 0.08 QWK of
the best configuration on every dataset at up to 30 times lower cost. Because every rule is keyed to
behaviour a user can check rather than to model identity, the same checks can be run on models we did
not test.

Future work follows from the gaps this study declares and from what the literature has yet to settle.
Two gaps arise in our design. Few-shot guidance, adding a few already-graded answers to the prompt as
examples, was held out; the strong effect of the studied guidance makes it worth testing, but only with
a principled rule for choosing the examples. And a fine-tuning arm remains open: fine-tuned models can
match or exceed earlier supervised graders~\cite{cong_memorization_2026}, but fine-tuning has not yet
been placed in a guide of this kind, comparing a fine-tuned grader against its base model on agreement,
consistency, and cost together. Three more follow from the literature. Code evidence rests on two
Java-centric datasets because code corpora graded with human rubrics are
scarce~\cite{havare_ai-based_2025,pathak_rubric_2025}, so widening it to other languages and paradigms,
and contributing such data, is the clearest way to firm up the code conclusions. Transfer was shown in a
single Portuguese deployment, so testing the rules across more institutions and student populations
would establish how far they carry. And the zero-collapse we found on code, where a grader scores many
partially correct answers as zero, is not, to our knowledge, described in the grading literature; it
deserves study in its own right, both to detect it and to prevent it without paying for reasoning.

For the instructor the introduction began with, the answer is a short routine: check candidate models for
zero-collapse, supply the rubric or reference answer, and grade one question per call with reasoning off,
for under one euro per thousand grades.
