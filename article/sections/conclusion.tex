\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusion}

An instructor adopting an LLM grader faces several choices at once, and prior studies addressed them
only in fragments, with some left unstudied. We brought those fragments together, tested them for
computer-science grading under one controlled protocol, and condensed the result into a configuration
guide. Four results carry the weight. Reasoning is a conditional trade: it raised agreement in at most
five of twenty model-dataset combinations, two after correcting for multiple comparisons, always at a
large token premium and usually at a cost in consistency, and its dependable success was repairing a
grader that collapsed to zero on code. Evaluation guidance is the cheapest reliable lever: it raised
agreement in eleven of twelve combinations across three model families. The shape of the request
matters less than its content: grading a whole exam in one call and scoring criterion by criterion never
improved agreement and cost the same or more. And the ranking of configurations on public data carried
over to a real deployment on code, and at the top on short answers. Together these define the guide's
default, the strongest model that does not collapse, with reasoning off, guidance, and one question per
call, which stayed within 0.08 QWK of the best configuration on every dataset at a fraction of its cost.
Because every prior is keyed to observable behaviour rather than to model identity, the guide is built
to outlast the models we tested.

Future work follows from the gaps this study declares and from what the literature has yet to settle.
Two gaps arise in our design. Few-shot guidance, adding a few already-graded answers to the prompt as
examples, was held out; the strong effect of the studied guidance makes it worth testing, but only with
a principled rule for choosing the examples. And a fine-tuning arm remains open: fine-tuning is already
competitive for grading~\cite{cong_memorization_2026}, but it has not yet been placed in a guide of this
kind, comparing a fine-tuned grader against its base model on agreement, consistency, and cost together.
Three more follow from the literature. Code evidence rests on two Java-centric datasets because
human-rubric-graded code corpora are scarce~\cite{havare_ai-based_2025,pathak_rubric_2025}, so widening
it to other languages and paradigms, and contributing such data, is the clearest way to firm up the code
conclusions. Transfer was shown in a single Portuguese deployment, so testing the priors across more
institutions and student populations would establish how far they carry. And the low-end score collapse
we found on code, where a grader floors much of the scale at zero, is not described in the grading
literature and deserves study in its own right, both to detect it and to prevent it without paying for
reasoning.
