\section{Study Design and Method}
\label{sec:method}

This section describes how the study was run: the configuration choices compared
(Section~\ref{sec:design}), the four datasets and their human grades (Section~\ref{sec:datasets}), the
models and serving backends (Section~\ref{sec:models}), the grading harness that drives every model
through one interface (Section~\ref{sec:harness}), and the metrics and statistical protocol
(Section~\ref{sec:metrics}). Every comparison changes one setting and holds everything else fixed, so
the comparisons are like-for-like.

\subsection{Configuration Dimensions}
\label{sec:design}

The study varies six configuration dimensions under one grading protocol (Table~\ref{tab:design}).
\emph{Reasoning} switches the model's thinking mode off or on. \emph{Evaluation guidance} controls what
grading material accompanies the question: the question stem and the student answer are always sent,
and the guidance level adds either nothing or the rubric (the scoring criteria) or reference answer the
dataset provides. \emph{Scope} contrasts grading a whole multi-question exam in one call against one
call per question. \emph{Decomposition} contrasts one holistic score against scoring each rubric
criterion separately and summing. \emph{Model family} covers three open-weight families and one closed
frontier model. \emph{Conversation state} contrasts a clean conversation per answer against a single
conversation that accumulates the grading history of one student's exam. In a shared conversation the
answers graded first can influence the ones graded later, so each exam is graded twice: once with the
answers in the exam's own question order and once in reverse order. Comparing the two isolates the
effect of an answer's position.

The \emph{baseline configuration} is reasoning off, with guidance, one question per call, holistic
scoring, and a clean conversation per answer. Model family and reasoning are crossed in full: every
family grades every dataset with reasoning off and on. The other dimensions are each varied one at a
time from the baseline, on two or three of the families: guidance on Qwen3.5, DeepSeek-V4-Flash, and
GPT-5.1; scope, decomposition, and conversation state on Qwen3.5 and DeepSeek-V4-Flash, with GLM-5.1
added for conversation state. Varying them one at a time keeps the number of runs affordable.

Two applicability rules follow from the data. First, scope needs complete multi-question exams, which
only PT-CS has, so it runs on PT-CS alone; decomposition needs rubrics with separately scored criteria,
which PT-CS and RIAYN both have. Second, guidance means different material on different datasets: a
question-specific rubric for PT-CS and RIAYN, and a reference answer for Mohler and SemEval. The
guidance effect is therefore two related interventions, which we report separately. PT-CS has no
reference solutions, and we did not synthesise any, to avoid introducing a confound.

\subsection{Datasets and Human Grades}
\label{sec:datasets}

Every comparison runs on three public datasets. PT-CS, our deployment dataset, repeats the comparisons
in a real deployment setting and alone supports the experiments that need complete exams. Throughout,
the \emph{human grade} is the grade a model's score is compared against. Because PT-CS holds both code
and short answers, which are graded differently, we analyse its two parts separately, so per-dataset
results cover five sets: Mohler, SemEval, RIAYN, PT-CS code, and PT-CS short answers.

\emph{Mohler}~\cite{mohler_text_2009,mohler_learning_2011} provides 2273 CS short answers over 81
questions, each graded 0 to 5 by two teachers; we use their mean. \emph{SemEval-2013
Task~7}~\cite{dzikovska_semeval_2013} (SciEntsBank and Beetle) provides science short answers whose
human grade is a binary label, correct or incorrect. SemEval is far larger than our other corpora, so we
subsample it: a fixed-seed random sample of 800 items, covering 228 of its 252 questions. The other datasets are graded in full. \emph{Rubric Is All You
Need} (RIAYN)~\cite{pathak_rubric_2025} provides 149 data-structures-and-algorithms submissions over 25
problems, all but one in Java. Its question-specific rubrics mark each step of one or more accepted
solution paths, and two graders scored each submission in consensus.

\emph{PT-CS} is our deployment dataset, built from a grading platform used in Portugal on which an AI
model proposes a grade for each answer, criterion by criterion, and two teachers review the proposal
and set the final grade jointly. PT-CS keeps the 379 answers whose final grade differs from the
AI-proposed total, which shows that the teachers changed it: 295 Java code answers and 84 short answers
on theory, in Portuguese, from 97 exams, each with a question-specific rubric and no reference solution.
Each question carries its own weight in the exam, between 1 and 12 points, so all scores are normalised
to a common scale before any analysis (Section~\ref{sec:metrics}). The data were pseudonymised: all
direct identifiers were removed, and every comment the platform had generated was stripped, so no model
output from the platform reaches the graders we test. The final grade is a joint decision with no
per-teacher record, so inter-rater agreement cannot be computed.

Two experiments need complete exams rather than single answers: whole-exam grading (scope) and the
conversation sub-study. Both grade complete exams from the same deployment. The scope contrast is scored
only on the exam questions that belong to PT-CS, and the conversation sub-study compares the model's
grades with one another, never with a teacher's, so it needs no validated grade.

\subsection{Models and Backends}
\label{sec:models}

Four model families grade every dataset, and all four let reasoning be switched on or off within the
same model, so the reasoning contrast holds the model fixed and flips only its thinking mode. Three are
open-weight: \emph{Qwen3.5}~\cite{qwen_qwen35_2026} (served as \texttt{Qwen/Qwen3.5-35B-A3B}),
\emph{DeepSeek-V4-Flash}~\cite{deepseek_v4_2026} (\texttt{deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V4-Flash}), and
\emph{GLM-5.1}~\cite{glm5_2026} (\texttt{zai-org/GLM-5.1}), all run via the DeepInfra inference
application programming interface (API) with \texttt{enable\_thinking} off or on. The fourth,
\emph{GPT-5.1}~\cite{openai_gpt51_2025} (via OpenAI, \texttt{reasoning\_effort} set to \texttt{none} or
\texttt{high}), is the closed frontier model; it grades the same items as the open models.

All calls use temperature 0, the setting that makes a model's output as repeatable as the backend
allows, so that the repeated runs measure the variation that remains rather than deliberate sampling
noise. Output is capped at 4096 tokens with reasoning off and at 32\,768 tokens with reasoning on.
Every run logs the provider, served model identifier, sampling parameters, prompt hash, and timestamp,
so each result's serving setup is recorded.

\subsection{Grading Harness}
\label{sec:harness}

A single grading harness, the software that runs every model through one common interface and records
its output, drives the whole study. Prompt templates are keyed by domain, guidance level, scope, and
decomposition; all templates request a machine-parseable JavaScript Object Notation (JSON) score, one
score per question for whole-exam calls and a per-criterion breakdown for criterion-level calls. A
deterministic parser extracts the grade and records whether extraction succeeded. We define the
\emph{extractable rate} $\pi$ as the fraction of runs whose output yields a parseable grade; unparseable
output is logged and excluded, never coerced to a zero score, because silent coercion fabricates
strictness. Each measurement is repeated $k=5$ times per item and configuration, and results are cached
by item, configuration, and repetition, so reruns never re-bill completed calls. Whole-exam calls grade
many questions in one API call, so tokens and cost are counted once per call.

The amount of grading balances statistical power against cost. With reasoning off, every model grades
the full public and PT-CS datasets (2273 Mohler, 149 RIAYN, 295 PT-CS code, and 84 PT-CS short-answer
items) and the 800-item SemEval sample, all at $k=5$. Reasoning on is the most expensive setting,
because reasoning traces run long, so it uses fixed random samples: 175 items on Mohler and SemEval, all
149 on RIAYN, and 67 code and 32 short-answer items on PT-CS. The whole-exam arm grades complete exams,
including the 88 that contain a validated code answer and cover all 295 PT-CS code questions; these
exams are short, about four questions each (one to six). The conversation sub-study grades 80 complete exams in
two fixed answer orders (the exam's own order and its reverse) with Qwen3.5 and DeepSeek-V4-Flash, and
the first 20 of those exams with GLM-5.1 at $k=3$.

\subsection{Metrics and Statistical Protocol}
\label{sec:metrics}

Two conventions are applied to every score before any metric. Predicted scores are clamped to
$[0, \text{scale max}]$, because rubrics with penalty criteria can push a criterion sum below zero and a
grade cannot be negative. Scores are then normalised to $[0,1]$ by their scale maximum, because the
native scales differ (Mohler 5, SemEval 1, PT-CS 1 to 12) and raw scores must never be pooled.

Agreement is reported as QWK, the standard measure for ordinal scoring: it
penalises large disagreements between grader and human grade more than small ones. It reaches 1 at
perfect agreement and 0 at chance, and can in principle fall below 0 when a grader disagrees more than
chance would; all the values we report are positive. QWK needs discrete, ordered grades, while the model
produces a continuous score. We therefore average each item's $k$ repetitions, round the mean onto six
ordered levels from 0 to 5, the resolution of Mohler's native grades, and compute QWK on these levels
(we write $K{=}5$ for this scale). Using the same scale for every dataset makes the QWK values
comparable. We fixed $K$ in advance, and recomputing with $K{=}4$ or $K{=}6$ changes no baseline QWK by
more than 0.05.

Consistency is the mean within-item standard deviation across the $k$ repetitions (SD$_k$); it needs no
human grade, and a lower value means more reproducible grading. The reasoning premium is the ratio of
output tokens with reasoning on to output tokens with reasoning off, computed within each model. Costs in
euros are computed from each call's logged input and output tokens at the per-token rates the providers
billed, ignoring discounts for cached input, so they are a slight upper bound. Rankings are compared with
Spearman's rank correlation $\rho$.

SemEval needs its own mapping as well, because its human grade is binary (correct or incorrect). We turn
the model's score into a correct/incorrect label at a fixed 0.5 cutoff, set in advance rather than tuned
to the data, because choosing the cutoff that maximised accuracy here would inflate the result. We then
report pooled accuracy together with AUROC, the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC)
curve, a threshold-free measure equal to the chance a correct answer outscores an incorrect one (0.5 is
chance, 1 is perfect). We do not break SemEval into its seen and unseen subsets: those splits were
defined for supervised systems relative to a training set, and our prompted models have none, so the
labels do not denote novelty to our grader.

Every expensive contrast is computed on its paired items, stated where the result is reported, and $N$
denotes the number of items a contrast rests on. The reasoning contrast pairs the off and on runs on the
sampled items and keeps the \emph{tractable} ones, whose reasoning stayed within the 32\,768-token limit,
so that every reasoning-on grade is complete. The limit cut off 293 graded runs on 186 items (91 SemEval,
65 Mohler, 17 RIAYN, 13 PT-CS), all from Qwen3.5 with reasoning on; all but three of the other runs in the
study returned a parseable grade, an extractable rate above 0.999. The scope contrast uses the 295 PT-CS code questions of the
whole-exam arm. Uncertainty is given by bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals (CIs) over items (800
resamples, fixed seed), and we call a contrast significant when its CI excludes zero. Because each
research question involves several comparisons, we also apply Holm's correction, which tightens the
significance threshold for each comparison so that the chance of any false positive within a group of
related contrasts stays at 5\%. The groups are reasoning, guidance, scope, and decomposition, and the
correction uses bootstrap $p$-values. A non-significant contrast does not show that two settings are
equivalent, so where equivalence matters we test it directly with the two one-sided tests (TOST)
procedure: two settings count as equivalent when the 90\% CI of their difference lies inside $\pm0.05$
QWK, a margin we fixed in advance as too small to change any recommendation. The conversation sub-study
uses paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, the standard nonparametric test for paired differences.
