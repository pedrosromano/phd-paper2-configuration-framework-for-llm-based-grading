\section{Study Design and Method}
\label{sec:method}

This section describes how the study was run: the configuration choices compared
(Section~\ref{sec:design}), the four datasets and the reliability of their reference grades
(Section~\ref{sec:datasets}), the models and serving backends (Section~\ref{sec:models}), the grading
harness that drives every model through one interface (Section~\ref{sec:harness}), and the metrics and
statistical protocol applied throughout (Section~\ref{sec:metrics}). Grading and scoring are identical across every model and configuration,
so that the comparisons the study draws are like-for-like.

\subsection{Configuration Dimensions}
\label{sec:design}

The study crosses six configuration dimensions under one grading protocol
(Table~\ref{tab:design}). \emph{Reasoning} switches the model's thinking mode off or on.
\emph{Evaluation guidance} controls what grading material accompanies the question: the question stem
and the student answer are always sent, and the guidance level adds either nothing or the rubric (the
scoring criteria) or reference answer the dataset provides. \emph{Scope} contrasts grading a whole multi-question exam in one call
against one call per question. \emph{Decomposition} contrasts one holistic score against scoring each
rubric criterion separately and summing. \emph{Model family} covers three open-weight families and one
closed frontier model. \emph{Conversation state} contrasts a clean conversation per answer against a
single conversation that accumulates the grading history of one student's exam, and is run as a
focused sub-study rather than a factorial dimension. In a shared conversation the answers graded first
can influence the ones graded later, so the sub-study grades each exam twice: once with the answers in
the exam's own question order and once in reverse order. Comparing the two isolates the effect of an
answer's position.

Model family and reasoning are crossed in full: every family grades every dataset with reasoning off
and on. Evaluation guidance, scope, and decomposition are each varied one at a time from a baseline
configuration, with reasoning off, on two or three of the families: guidance on Qwen3.5,
DeepSeek-V4-Flash, and GPT-5.1, and scope and decomposition on Qwen3.5 and DeepSeek-V4-Flash. Varying
them one at a time keeps the number of runs affordable (Section~\ref{sec:harness}).

Two applicability rules keep the design honest. First, scope and decomposition exist only where the
data support them. Scope needs complete multi-question exams, which only PT-CS has, so it runs on PT-CS
alone; decomposition needs rubrics with separately scored criteria, which PT-CS and RIAYN both have.
Second, guidance means different material in the
two domains: a question-specific rubric for the code datasets (PT-CS, RIAYN) and a reference answer
for the short-answer datasets (Mohler, SemEval). The guidance effect is therefore two related
interventions, not one, and we never pool the two. PT-CS has no reference
solutions, and we did not synthesise any, to avoid introducing a confound.

\subsection{Datasets and Ground Truth}
\label{sec:datasets}

We develop every comparison on three public datasets and reserve PT-CS, our deployment dataset, for
transfer validation and for the experiments only it can support, which need its multi-question
exams and rubric criteria.
Throughout, the \emph{gold} grade is the human grade that a model's score is compared against.
\emph{Mohler}~\cite{mohler_text_2009,mohler_learning_2011} provides 2273 CS short answers over 80
questions, each graded 0 to 5 by two teachers (we use their mean). The two annotators themselves agree
only moderately~\cite{mohler_learning_2011}, so agreement against their mean carries a human ceiling
that we keep in mind when reading absolute Mohler scores. \emph{SemEval-2013
Task~7}~\cite{dzikovska_semeval_2013} (SciEntsBank and Beetle) provides short answers whose gold grade is
a binary label, correct or incorrect.
SemEval is far larger than our other corpora, so it is the one we subsample: we draw 800 items by a
fixed-seed sample that preserves the dataset's question and domain proportions. The other public
datasets are graded in full. \emph{Rubric Is All You Need}
(RIAYN)~\cite{pathak_rubric_2025} provides 149 data-structures-and-algorithms submissions over 25
problems, all but one in Java, with question-specific rubrics that mark each step of one or more
accepted solution paths, graded by two graders in consensus; it is our public code comparator. Published results situate our numbers but are not the success criterion
(Table~\ref{tab:published-baselines}): they come from different models, prompts, and protocols, so
all conclusions here rest on our own like-for-like contrasts.

\emph{PT-CS} is our deployment dataset, built from a real AI-assisted grading platform used in
Portugal. On this platform an artificial-intelligence (AI) model proposes a grade for each answer,
criterion by criterion, and two teachers review the proposal and set the final grade jointly. PT-CS
contains the 379 answers whose grade the teachers validated in this way, which the records show because
the final grade departs from the proposed total: 295 Java code answers and 84 theory short answers, in
Portuguese, from 97 exams, each with a question-specific rubric and no reference solution. Each question
carries its own weight in the exam, between 1 and 12 points, so all scores are normalised to a common
scale before any analysis (Section~\ref{sec:metrics}). The data were pseudonymised: all direct
identifiers were removed, and every comment the platform had generated was stripped, so no model output
from the platform reaches the graders we test. The final grade is a joint decision with no per-teacher
record, so inter-rater agreement cannot be computed.

Two experiments need complete exams rather than single answers: whole-exam grading (scope) and the
conversation sub-study. Both grade complete exams from the same deployment. The scope contrast is scored
only on the exam questions that belong to PT-CS, and the conversation sub-study compares the model's
grades with one another, never with a teacher's, so it needs no validated grade (Section~\ref{sec:rq5}).

\subsection{Models and Backends}
\label{sec:models}

Four model families grade every dataset. Three are open-weight and expose a within-family reasoning
toggle, so the reasoning contrast holds the model fixed and flips only its thinking mode:
\emph{Qwen3.5}~\cite{qwen_qwen35_2026} (served as \texttt{Qwen/Qwen3.5-35B-A3B}),
\emph{DeepSeek-V4-Flash}~\cite{deepseek_v4_2026} (\texttt{deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V4-Flash}), and
\emph{GLM-5.1}~\cite{glm5_2026} (\texttt{zai-org/GLM-5.1}), all run via the DeepInfra inference
application programming interface (API) with \texttt{enable\_thinking} off or on. The fourth,
\emph{GPT-5.1}~\cite{openai_gpt51_2025} (via OpenAI, \texttt{reasoning\_effort} mapped to
\texttt{none} or \texttt{high}), is the closed frontier model; it grades the same items as the open
models. Qwen3.5 with reasoning off, with guidance, question-by-question, and holistic scoring is the
internal baseline configuration against which the dimension contrasts are framed.

All calls use temperature 0, the setting that makes a model's output as repeatable as the backend
allows, so that the repeated runs measure the variation that remains rather than deliberate sampling
noise. Output is capped at 4096 tokens with reasoning off and at 32\,768 tokens with reasoning on.
Every run logs the provider, served model identifier, sampling parameters, prompt hash, and timestamp.
We log these details because absolute scores are conditional on the full serving setup, not just the prompt
and sampling we control: the served quantization, the inference software, and the hardware differ
across providers and cannot be matched exactly, so a different setup can yield different absolute
scores. The transferable claim is the configuration guidance (which configuration beats which), not
the absolute scores.

\subsection{Grading Harness}
\label{sec:harness}

A single grading harness, the software that runs every model through one common interface and records
its output, drives the whole study. Prompt templates are keyed by domain,
guidance level, scope, and decomposition; all templates request a machine-parseable JSON (JavaScript Object Notation) score, one
score per question for whole-exam calls and a per-criterion breakdown for criterion-level calls. A
deterministic parser extracts the grade and records whether extraction succeeded. We define the
\emph{extractable rate} $\pi$ as the fraction of runs whose output yields a parseable grade;
unparseable output is logged and excluded, never coerced to a zero score, because silent coercion
fabricates strictness. Each measurement is repeated $k=5$ times per item and configuration, and results
are cached by item, configuration hash, and repetition index, so reruns
never re-bill completed calls. Whole-exam calls grade many questions in one
API call, so token and cost are counted once per call, not once per question.

We call the full set of model, configuration, and dataset combinations that were run the
\emph{experiment matrix}, and it balances statistical power against cost. With reasoning off, every
model grades the full public and PT-CS datasets (2273 Mohler, 149 RIAYN, 295 PT-CS code, and 84
PT-CS short-answer items) and the 800-item SemEval sample, all at $k=5$. Reasoning on is the most
expensive setting, because reasoning traces run long, so it uses fixed random samples: 175 items per
public dataset, and 67 code and 32 short-answer items on PT-CS. The whole-exam arm grades the 88
complete exams that contain a validated code answer, covering all 295 PT-CS code questions. The
conversation sub-study grades 80 complete exams in two fixed answer orders (the exam's own order and its
reverse) with Qwen3.5 and DeepSeek-V4-Flash, and the first 20 of those exams with GLM-5.1 at $k=3$, with
everything else frozen at the baseline configuration. We did not run every combination. Model family
and reasoning are fully crossed: each of the four families grades with reasoning off and on. The other
dimension contrasts (evaluation guidance, scope, and decomposition) are secondary to the reasoning
question, so each was varied on its own from the baseline configuration, with reasoning off, on two or
three families rather than crossed with every setting, which would multiply the matrix. We therefore
report each of these effects on its own, not how two of them combine, and we make no claims about
combined effects.

\subsection{Metrics and Statistical Protocol}
\label{sec:metrics}

Two conventions are applied to every score before any metric. Predicted scores are clamped to
$[0, \text{scale max}]$, because rubrics with penalty criteria can push a criterion sum below zero
(88 such rows) and a grade cannot be negative. Scores are then normalised to $[0,1]$ by their scale
maximum, because the native scales differ (Mohler 5, SemEval 1, PT-CS 1 to 12) and raw scores must
never be pooled.

Agreement is reported primarily as QWK, the standard measure for ordinal scoring: it penalises large grader-reference disagreements more than small ones. It reaches 1 at
perfect agreement and 0 at chance, and can in principle fall below 0 when a grader disagrees with the
reference more than chance would; all the values we report are positive. QWK needs discrete, ordered grades, but the model
produces a continuous score, so we first round each normalised score onto a common 0-to-5 ordinal
scale ($K{=}5$), the resolution of Mohler's native grades. Using the same scale for every dataset
makes the QWK values comparable. We fixed $K$ in advance, and recomputing with $K{=}4$ or $K{=}6$
changes no baseline QWK by more than 0.033, so the choice does not drive the results. QWK is then
computed on the mean of each item's $k$ repetitions.

Alongside QWK we report mean absolute error (MAE) on the normalised score, the average grading error
as a fraction of the scale, and Spearman rank correlation. Consistency is the mean within-item
standard deviation across the $k$ repetitions (SD$_k$); it needs no reference, and a lower value means
more reproducible grading. The reasoning premium is measured in output tokens, as the on/off ratio
within each provider, since DeepInfra and OpenAI account for reasoning tokens differently. Costs in euros
are computed from each call's logged input and output tokens at the per-token rates the providers billed,
ignoring discounts for cached input, so they are a slight upper bound.

SemEval needs its own mapping, because the model emits a continuous score while its gold is binary
(correct or incorrect). We turn the score into a correct/incorrect label at a fixed 0.5 cutoff, set in
advance rather than tuned to the data, because choosing the cutoff that maximised accuracy here would
inflate the result. We then report pooled accuracy together with AUROC, the area under the receiver operating
characteristic (ROC) curve, a threshold-free measure equal to the chance a correct answer outscores an incorrect one (0.5 is
chance, 1 is perfect); it confirms the result does not hinge on the 0.5 cut.
We do not break SemEval into its seen and unseen subsets: those splits were defined for supervised
systems relative to a training set, and our prompted models have none, so the labels do not denote
novelty to our grader.

Every expensive contrast is computed on its paired subset, stated where the result is reported: the
reasoning contrast pairs the off and on runs on the sampled items (restricted to those whose
reasoning never overflowed the token ceiling, Section~\ref{sec:rq1}) and the scope contrast on the 295
PT-CS code questions of the whole-exam arm; $N$ denotes the number of items a contrast rests on.
Uncertainty is given by bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals (CIs) over items (800 resamples, fixed seed),
and we call a contrast significant when its CI excludes zero. We report effect sizes with CIs and, because
each research question involves several comparisons, we also apply Holm's correction for multiple
comparisons within each family of contrasts (reasoning, guidance, scope and decomposition), using
bootstrap $p$-values, and report which results survive it. A non-significant contrast does not show that
two settings are equivalent, so where equivalence matters we test it directly: two settings count as
equivalent when the 90\% CI of their difference lies inside $\pm0.05$ QWK, a margin we fixed in advance
as too small to change any recommendation (a two one-sided test, TOST). The conversation
sub-study uses paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, the standard nonparametric test for paired
differences.
