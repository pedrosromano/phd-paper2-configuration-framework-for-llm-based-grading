\section{Study Design and Method}
\label{sec:method}

We graded the answers of four datasets with four model families under one grading protocol and compared
every grade with the grade a teacher gave. Starting from a baseline configuration, each comparison
changes one configuration choice and keeps everything else fixed. Every answer is graded five times
with each configuration, so agreement with the teachers, consistency, and cost are measured together.

\subsection{Configuration Choices}
\label{sec:design}

The study varies seven configuration choices (Table~\ref{tab:design}). \emph{Reasoning} switches the
model's thinking mode off or on. \emph{Evaluation guidance} is the grading material added to the
question and the student answer, which are always sent: nothing, or the rubric (the scoring criteria)
or the reference answer that the dataset provides. The \emph{grading instruction} tells the model how
to apply that guidance: strictly, as the rubric specifies, or awarding partial credit explicitly for
every rubric item or part of the reference answer that the student answer meets, optionally with a
justification of at most two sentences written before the score. \emph{Scope} contrasts grading a whole
multi-question exam in one call against one call per question. \emph{Decomposition} contrasts one
holistic score against scoring each rubric criterion separately and summing. \emph{Conversation state}
contrasts a clean conversation per answer against a single conversation that accumulates the grading
history of one student's exam. In a shared conversation the answers graded first can influence the ones
graded later, so each exam is graded twice: once with the answers in the exam's own question order and
once in reverse order. Comparing the two isolates the effect of an answer's position. The last choice is
the model.

The \emph{baseline configuration} is reasoning off, with guidance, the strict instruction, one question
per call, holistic scoring, and a clean conversation per answer. Model and reasoning are crossed in
full: every model grades every dataset with reasoning off and on. Each other choice is changed one at a
time from the baseline, on the models listed in Table~\ref{tab:design}.

Three applicability rules follow from the data. Scope needs complete multi-question exams, which only
PT-CS has. Decomposition needs rubrics with separately scored criteria, which PT-CS and RIAYN both have.
Guidance means different material on different datasets: a question-specific rubric for PT-CS and RIAYN,
and a reference answer for Mohler and SemEval, so the two are reported separately. PT-CS has no reference
solutions, and we did not write any, to avoid introducing a confound.

\subsection{Datasets and Human Grades}
\label{sec:datasets}

Table~\ref{tab:datasets} summarises the four datasets. Throughout, the \emph{human grade} is the grade a
model's score is compared against. PT-CS holds both code and short answers, which are graded
differently, so we analyse its two parts separately, and per-dataset results cover five sets: Mohler,
SemEval, RIAYN, PT-CS code, and PT-CS short answers.

\input{tables/tab_datasets}

\emph{Mohler}~\cite{mohler_text_2009,mohler_learning_2011} provides CS short answers, each graded 0 to 5
by two teachers; we use their mean. \emph{SemEval-2013 Task~7}~\cite{dzikovska_semeval_2013}
(SciEntsBank and Beetle) provides science short answers whose human grade is a binary label, correct or
incorrect. It is far larger than our other corpora, so we use a fixed-seed random sample of 800 answers.
\emph{Rubric Is All You Need} (RIAYN)~\cite{pathak_rubric_2025} provides data-structures-and-algorithms
submissions, all but one in Java, whose question-specific rubrics mark each step of one or more accepted
solution paths; two graders scored each submission in consensus.

\emph{PT-CS} is a new dataset that we collected with a proof-of-concept grading platform, built within
this research project as a first prototype for gathering graded answers. It holds complete Java exams in
Portuguese, and each question has its own rubric and no reference solution. Two teachers set the final
grade of each answer jointly. PT-CS keeps the 379 answers whose final grade the two teachers changed from
the score the platform first suggested: 295 code answers and 84 short answers on theory, from 97 exams.
All direct identifiers were removed. The final grade is a joint decision with no per-teacher record, so
inter-rater agreement cannot be computed on PT-CS.

Two experiments need complete exams rather than single answers: whole-exam grading (scope) and the
conversation sub-study. The scope contrast is scored only on the exam questions among the 379
teacher-validated answers, and the conversation sub-study compares the model's grades with one another,
never with a teacher's, so it needs no human grade.

\subsection{Models and Backends}
\label{sec:models}

The models were chosen for three properties. Each lets reasoning be switched on or off within the same
model, so the reasoning comparison holds the model fixed and flips only its thinking mode. The three
open-weight families come from three different developers and were among the strongest open models
when the study began. The closed model is the lowest-priced of the closed frontier models whose
reasoning can be switched fully off, and with it the prices span more than two orders of magnitude.
The open-weight models are \emph{Qwen3.5}~\cite{qwen_qwen35_2026} (served as
\texttt{Qwen/Qwen3.5-35B-A3B}), \emph{DeepSeek-V4-Flash}~\cite{deepseek_v4_2026}
(\texttt{deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V4-Flash}), and \emph{GLM-5.1}~\cite{glm5_2026}
(\texttt{zai-org/GLM-5.1}), all run via the DeepInfra inference application programming interface (API)
with \texttt{enable\_thinking} off or on. The closed model is \emph{GPT-5.1}~\cite{openai_gpt51_2025}
(via OpenAI, \texttt{reasoning\_effort} set to \texttt{none} or \texttt{high}). DeepInfra no longer offers
GLM-5.1 with reasoning off, so guidance, scope, and decomposition are compared on the other three models,
and in the grading-instruction comparison GLM-5.1 is served by its developer, Z.ai, through OpenRouter, with
reasoning disabled. That comparison also includes a second closed model, \emph{Claude Sonnet 4.6}
(\texttt{claude-sonnet-4-6}, via Anthropic, reasoning off), whose answer is constrained by the API's
structured output to the JSON score that every prompt asks for; without that constraint it sometimes wrote
an analysis before the score.

All calls use temperature 0, the setting that makes a model's output as repeatable as the backend
allows, so that the repeated runs measure only the variation that remains at that setting. Output is
capped at 4096 tokens with reasoning off and at 32\,768 tokens with reasoning on. Every run logs the
provider, served model identifier, sampling parameters, prompt hash, and timestamp.

\subsection{Grading Harness}
\label{sec:harness}

A single grading harness, the software that runs every model through one common interface and records
its output, drives the whole study. Prompt templates are keyed by domain, guidance level, grading
instruction, scope, and decomposition. All templates ask for the score as a JavaScript Object Notation
(JSON) object, with one score per question for whole-exam calls and a score per criterion for
criterion-level calls. A deterministic parser extracts the grade and records whether extraction
succeeded; unparseable output is logged and left out of the metrics.

With reasoning off, every model grades every answer of every dataset five times ($k=5$). Reasoning on
is the most expensive setting, because reasoning traces run long, so it uses the fixed random samples of
Table~\ref{tab:datasets}, also graded five times. The whole-exam arm grades the 88 complete exams that
contain a teacher-validated code answer, about four questions each (one to six). The conversation
sub-study grades 80 complete exams in both answer orders, five times each with Qwen3.5 and
DeepSeek-V4-Flash, and the first 20 of those exams three times with GLM-5.1.

\subsection{Metrics and Statistics}
\label{sec:metrics}

Two conventions are applied to every score before any metric. Predicted scores are clamped to
$[0, \text{scale max}]$, because rubrics with penalty criteria can push a criterion sum below zero and a
grade cannot be negative. Scores are then normalised to $[0,1]$ by their scale maximum, because the
native scales differ (Mohler 5, SemEval 1, PT-CS 1 to 12) and raw scores must never be pooled.

Agreement is reported as quadratic weighted kappa (QWK), the standard measure for ordinal scoring: it
penalises large disagreements between grader and human grade more than small ones. It reaches 1 at
perfect agreement and 0 at chance, and can in principle fall below 0 when a grader disagrees more than
chance would; all the values we report are positive. QWK needs discrete, ordered grades, while the model
produces a continuous score. We therefore average each item's $k$ repetitions, round the mean onto six
ordered levels from 0 to 5, the resolution of Mohler's native grades, and compute QWK on these levels
(we write $K{=}5$ for this scale). Using the same scale for every dataset makes the QWK values
comparable. We fixed $K$ in advance, and recomputing with $K{=}4$ or $K{=}6$ changes no baseline QWK by
more than 0.05.

Consistency is the mean within-item standard deviation across the $k$ repetitions (SD$_k$); it needs no
human grade, and a lower value means more reproducible grading. The reasoning premium is the ratio of
output tokens with reasoning on to output tokens with reasoning off, computed within each model. Costs in
euros are computed from each call's logged input and output tokens at the providers' standard per-token
rates, ignoring discounts for cached input, so they are a slight upper bound. Rank correlations use
Spearman's $\rho$.

SemEval's human grade is binary, so we also turn the model's score into a correct or incorrect label at
a fixed cutoff of 0.5, set in advance, and report accuracy, the share of answers labelled as the
teachers labelled them. We do not break SemEval into its seen and unseen subsets: those splits were
defined for supervised systems relative to a training set, and our prompted models have none, so the
labels do not denote novelty to our grader.

Every contrast is computed on paired items, and $N$ denotes the number of items it rests on. The
reasoning contrast pairs the runs with reasoning off and on over the sampled items. When a reasoning run
reached the output limit without returning a grade, it takes the grade the same model gave with
reasoning off in the same repetition, as a grading system would fall back to a cheaper call;
Section~\ref{sec:rq1} reports how often this happened. The scope contrast uses the 295 PT-CS code
questions of the whole-exam arm.

To show how precise each difference is, we report a 95\% confidence interval (CI) obtained by resampling
the items with replacement 800 times (a bootstrap), and we call a difference significant when its
interval excludes zero. Because each research question involves several comparisons, we also apply
Holm's correction, which tightens the significance threshold for each comparison so that the chance of
any false positive within a group of related contrasts stays at 5\%. The groups are reasoning, guidance,
grading instruction, scope, and decomposition, and the correction uses bootstrap $p$-values. A
non-significant difference does not show that two settings are equivalent, so where equivalence matters
we test it directly with the two one-sided tests (TOST) procedure: two settings count as equivalent when
the 90\% CI of their difference lies inside $\pm0.05$ QWK, a margin we fixed in advance as too small to
change any recommendation. The conversation sub-study uses paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, the
standard nonparametric test for paired differences.
