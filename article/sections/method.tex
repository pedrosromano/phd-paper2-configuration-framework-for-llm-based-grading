\section{Study Design and Method}
\label{sec:method}

We graded the answers of four datasets with five LLMs (Qwen3.5, DeepSeek-V4-Flash, GLM-5.1, GPT-5.1,
and Claude Sonnet 4.6) under one grading protocol and compared every grade with the grade a teacher
gave. Starting from a baseline configuration, each comparison changes one configuration choice and keeps
everything else fixed. Each answer is graded $k$ times in every configuration it enters, with $k=5$
unless stated otherwise, so agreement with the human graders, consistency across the $k$ repetitions, and
cost are measured together.

\subsection{Configuration Choices}
\label{sec:design}

The study varies seven configuration choices (Table~\ref{tab:design}), one of them, the grading
instruction, in a follow-up experiment added because the first results showed some models scoring many
partially correct code answers as zero. \emph{Reasoning} switches the
model's thinking mode off or on. \emph{Evaluation guidance} is the grading material added to the
question and the student answer, which are always sent: nothing, or the rubric (the scoring criteria)
or the reference answer that the dataset provides. The \emph{grading instruction} tells the model how
to apply that guidance: strictly, as the rubric specifies, or awarding partial credit explicitly for
every rubric item or part of the reference answer that the student answer meets, optionally with a
justification of at most two sentences written before the score. \emph{Scope} contrasts grading a whole
multi-question exam in one call against one call per question. \emph{Decomposition} contrasts one
holistic score (a single overall grade for the answer) with a score for each rubric criterion followed by the total. The grade is the total the
model gives, not the sum of its criterion scores, because some rubrics describe alternative solutions and
only the criteria of the solution the student wrote should count. \emph{Conversation state}
contrasts a clean conversation per answer against a single conversation that accumulates the grading
history of one student's exam. In a shared conversation the answers graded first can influence the ones
graded later, so each exam is graded twice: once with the answers in the exam's own question order and
once in reverse order. Comparing the two isolates the effect of an answer's position. The last choice is
the \emph{model}.

The \emph{baseline configuration} is reasoning off, with guidance, the strict instruction, one question
per call, holistic scoring, and a clean conversation per answer. Model and reasoning are crossed in
full: Qwen3.5, DeepSeek-V4-Flash, GLM-5.1, and GPT-5.1 grade every dataset with reasoning off and on.
Each other choice is changed one at a
time from the baseline, on the models listed in Table~\ref{tab:design}. Each choice was compared on at least three of the five
models. Claude Sonnet 4.6 joined in the follow-up experiment as a second closed model and was graded with
reasoning off, the setting the results favoured; with reasoning on it would have been the costliest
configuration of the study. GLM-5.1 is absent from the guidance, scope, and decomposition comparisons
because the provider used for it stopped offering it with reasoning off before they were run.

Three applicability rules follow from the data. Scope and conversation state need complete
multi-question exams, which only PT-CS has. Decomposition needs rubrics with separately scored criteria,
which PT-CS and RIAYN both have.
Guidance means different material on different datasets: a question-specific rubric for PT-CS and RIAYN,
and a reference answer for Mohler and SemEval, so the two are reported separately. PT-CS has no reference
solutions, and we did not write any, to avoid introducing a confound.

\subsection{Datasets and Human Grades}
\label{sec:datasets}

Table~\ref{tab:datasets} summarises the four datasets. Throughout, the \emph{human grade} is the grade a
model's score is compared against. PT-CS holds both code and short answers, which are graded
differently, so we analyse its two parts separately, and per-dataset results cover five sets: Mohler,
SemEval, RIAYN, PT-CS code, and PT-CS short answers.

\emph{Mohler}~\cite{mohler_text_2009,mohler_learning_2011} provides CS short answers, each graded 0 to 5
by two teachers; we use their mean. \emph{SemEval-2013 Task~7}~\cite{dzikovska_semeval_2013}
(SciEntsBank and Beetle) provides science short answers whose human grade is a binary label, correct or
incorrect; it is the most widely used short-answer benchmark, and its subject checks that the short-answer
findings are not specific to CS. It is far larger than our other corpora, so we use a fixed-seed random
sample of 800 answers from all its subsets, with every reference answer a question has.
\emph{Rubric Is All You Need} (RIAYN)~\cite{pathak_rubric_2025} provides data-structures-and-algorithms
submissions to a practice website (149 of 150; one lacks a grade), 148 in Java and one in Python, whose question-specific rubrics mark each step of
one or more accepted solution paths; two graders scored each submission in consensus.

\emph{PT-CS} consists of 97 complete Java exams in Portuguese from a course at Iscte, with 634 answers.
Each question has its own rubric and no reference solution. Two of the authors, who taught the course,
manually graded 379 of these answers, those whose final grade they set by hand, as recorded on the platform
(295 code answers and 84 short answers on theory), before this study was designed; these are PT-CS's human grades. Each is a joint decision with no per-teacher record, so
inter-rater agreement cannot be computed on PT-CS. The grades recorded for the other answers could not be
verified, so they are not used. Those answers appear only in the two experiments that need whole exams,
grading a whole exam in one call and the conversation sub-study: keeping them is the only way to grade
complete exams, but their grades are never used.

For the four models graded with reasoning both off and on, reasoning is switched within the same model,
so the reasoning comparison holds the model fixed and flips only its thinking mode; Claude Sonnet 4.6,
added in the follow-up experiment, was graded with reasoning off only. The model families follow prior studies of LLM
grading of student answers, in which Claude~\cite{cipriano_leveraging_2025},
GPT~\cite{li_study_2025,sessler_canai_2025}, DeepSeek~\cite{aminah_evaluating_2025,li_study_2025},
and Qwen~\cite{jiang_eipe_2026,havare_ai-based_2025} models were among the best graders tested.
GLM-5.1, without a comparable grading record, was added as the strongest open model for code when the
study began~\cite{arena_leaderboard_2026}. GPT-5.1 was chosen as a closed frontier model whose reasoning
can be switched fully off within the same model, which Gemini 3 Pro does not allow, and with it the prices
span more than two orders of magnitude.
The open-weight models are \emph{Qwen3.5-35B-A3B}~\cite{qwen_qwen35_2026} (\texttt{Qwen/Qwen3.5-35B-A3B},
called Qwen3.5 below), a small mixture-of-experts model that activates about 3B of its 35B parameters for
each token, \emph{DeepSeek-V4-Flash}~\cite{deepseek_v4_2026}
(\texttt{deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V4-Flash}), and \emph{GLM-5.1}~\cite{glm5_2026}
(\texttt{zai-org/GLM-5.1}), run via the DeepInfra inference application programming interface (API)
with \texttt{enable\_thinking} off or on, and GLM-5.1 also via OpenRouter. The closed models are
\emph{GPT-5.1}~\cite{openai_gpt51_2025} (via OpenAI, \texttt{reasoning\_effort} set to \texttt{none} or \texttt{high}, its highest level) and \emph{Claude Sonnet
4.6}~\cite{anthropic_sonnet46_2026} (\texttt{claude-sonnet-4-6}, via Anthropic, reasoning off). The API's
structured output restricts Claude Sonnet 4.6's answer to the score in JavaScript Object Notation (JSON)
that every prompt asks for, so
that, like the other models with reasoning off, it returns the score without writing an analysis first.

Calls to the open models and to Claude Sonnet 4.6 use temperature 0, the setting that makes a model's
output as repeatable as the backend allows, so that the repeated runs measure only the variation that
remains at that setting. GPT-5.1 was called without a temperature setting and samples at its default, so its
consistency is best compared within the model rather than against the others. Output
is capped at 4096 tokens with reasoning off and at 32\,768 tokens with reasoning on. Every run logs the
provider, served model identifier, sampling parameters, prompt hash, and timestamp.

\subsection{Grading Harness}
\label{sec:harness}

A single grading harness, the software that runs every model through one common interface and records
its output, drives the whole study. Prompt templates are keyed by domain, guidance level, grading
instruction, scope, and decomposition. They are written in English for every dataset; the PT-CS
questions, rubrics, and answers stay in Portuguese. All templates ask for the score as a JSON object, with one score per question for whole-exam calls and a score per criterion for
criterion-level calls. A deterministic parser extracts the grade and records whether extraction
succeeded; unparseable output is logged and left out of the metrics. Without reasoning, at most 0.6\% of
any configuration's outputs could not be parsed. The supplementary material gives every prompt template,
the models' identifiers and prices, the unparseable outputs per model, and the values behind every figure of this paper.

With reasoning off, every model grades every answer of every dataset $k=5$ times, with two exceptions
in the follow-up experiment: Claude Sonnet 4.6, the costliest model without reasoning, grades
a fixed random 800 of the 2273 Mohler answers, and GLM-5.1 grades only the code sets. Reasoning on is the
most expensive setting, because reasoning traces run long, so it grades fixed random samples
(Table~\ref{tab:datasets}): all 149 RIAYN answers and 32 to 175 answers on the other sets. The
whole-exam arm grades the 88 exams that contain at least one of the 295 PT-CS code answers, about four
questions each (one to six). The conversation sub-study grades the 97 exams, in both answer orders, with Qwen3.5,
DeepSeek-V4-Flash, and GLM-5.1.

\subsection{Metrics and Statistics}
\label{sec:metrics}

Every grade, the teacher's and the model's, is divided by the question's maximum mark, so all grades lie
between 0 and 1 whatever the dataset's scale. A Mohler question is worth 5 points, a SemEval answer is
either correct or incorrect (1 or 0), and a question is worth 4 to 12 points in RIAYN and 1 to 12 in
PT-CS. A model score below zero, which arose when a rubric had penalty criteria and the model applied
only a penalty, is set to zero.

Agreement is reported as quadratic weighted kappa (QWK), the standard measure for ordinal scoring: it
penalises large disagreements between grader and human grade more than small ones. It reaches 1 at
perfect agreement and 0 at chance, and can in principle fall below 0 when a grader disagrees more than
chance would; all the values we report are positive. QWK needs discrete, ordered grades, while the model
produces a continuous score. We therefore average each item's $k$ grades, round the mean to the nearest of
six ordered levels (0 to 5, the points of a Mohler question; a value exactly halfway between two
levels goes to the even one), and compute QWK on these levels.
Using the same six levels for every dataset makes the QWK values comparable; recomputing on five or seven
levels changes no baseline QWK by more than 0.06.

Consistency is the mean within-item standard deviation across the $k$ repetitions (SD$_k$); it needs no
human grade, and a lower value means more reproducible grading. The reasoning premium is the ratio of
output tokens with reasoning on to output tokens with reasoning off, computed within each model. Costs in
euros are computed from each call's logged input and output tokens at the providers' standard per-token
rates, ignoring discounts for cached input or batched requests, so they are an upper bound; at these
rates, all the experiments together cost \euro{}211.4. Rank correlations use Spearman's $\rho$.

SemEval's human grade is binary, so we also turn the model's score into a correct or incorrect label at
a fixed cutoff of 0.5, set in advance, and report accuracy, the share of answers labelled as the
human graders labelled them. We do not break SemEval into its seen and unseen subsets: those splits were
defined for supervised systems relative to a training set, and our prompted models have none, so the
labels do not denote novelty to our grader.

Every contrast compares two configurations on the same items, and $N$ denotes the number of items it
rests on. The reasoning contrast pairs the runs with reasoning off and on over the sampled items. Some
reasoning runs reached the output limit without returning a grade; to keep these hard answers in the
comparison, each such run takes the grade the same model gave that answer with reasoning off, as a grading
system would retry with a cheaper call. The scope contrast uses the 295 PT-CS code questions of the whole-exam arm.

We report each difference with a 95\% confidence interval (CI), obtained by resampling the items with
replacement 10,000 times (a bootstrap). A difference is significant when its interval excludes zero; its
p-value is twice the share of resamples that fall on the other side of zero. Answers to the same question
may be alike, so we also repeated every comparison resampling whole questions, and computed QWK on single
runs as well as on the mean of the $k$ grades (supplementary material). Each
research question involves several comparisons, so we also apply Holm's correction within each group of
related comparisons (reasoning, guidance, grading instruction, scope, and decomposition). The correction
raises the bar for each comparison so that the chance of any false positive in the group stays at 5\%. A
non-significant difference does not show that two settings are equivalent. Where equivalence matters, we
test it directly: two settings count as equivalent when the 90\% CI of their difference lies within
$\pm0.05$ QWK (the two one-sided tests procedure, TOST), a margin we fixed in advance as too small to
change any recommendation. The conversation sub-study compares two grades of the same answer; its intervals
resample whole exams, because the answers of one exam, graded in one conversation, are not independent.

\subsection{Configuration Guide Validation}
\label{sec:validmethod}

%% Protocol fixed before any call (CLAUDE.md S11, 2026-10-18, with two amendments made before any comparison);

%% for these two datasets, which played no part in building the guide.
The tests of the configuration guide use the answers it was built from, so we also graded two datasets that
played no part in building it, as validation datasets. \emph{SAF}~\cite{filighera_saf_2022} provides English short answers on
communication networks, each scored with partial credit by two trained annotators in consensus and paired
with a reference answer; we use a fixed-seed random sample of 300 of its 2981 answers~\cite{saf_release_2022}, covering
its 31 questions. \emph{HARMOGEN-R}~\cite{mendonca_harmogenr_2026} provides C++ code answers from a data-structures
course, in Portuguese, each graded by four instructors independently and blind with their own rubric of
separately scored criteria, the only rubric we use (the dataset also includes rubrics written by LLMs); we use
all 129 non-empty answers to its three code questions, with the instructors' mean as the human grade. On both, the guide's configuration is compared with alternatives that
each change one of its settings: no guidance, the strict instruction in place of the request for partial
credit, reasoning on, and, on code, a score per criterion. The models grade every configuration three
times, with two exceptions: Claude Sonnet 4.6 grades with reasoning off and one holistic score, as in the
study, and reasoning on grades the first 150 of the 300 SAF answers in their fixed random order. As in the main comparison, a reasoning run that
returned no grade is kept, with the grade the guide (reasoning off) gave that answer. The comparisons use the
metric and bootstrap described above. Before any of
these answers was graded, we fixed the criterion the guide had to meet: on each dataset, no alternative
without reasoning may agree with the human grades significantly better than the guide, for any model.
