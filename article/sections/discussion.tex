\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Read together, the six questions return one message: in LLM grading almost nothing is unconditional.
Whether a configuration choice helps depends on the task at hand, the model's behaviour, the reliability
of the grades used as reference, and which quality is being optimised, whether agreement, consistency, or
cost. The paragraphs below draw the findings out along these lines and then take up what they imply for
choosing a configuration.

Reasoning was the clearest conditional effect. Extended thinking improved agreement with teacher grades
in only a minority of model-dataset combinations, five of twenty and two after correcting for multiple
comparisons, and it always came with a high price. It multiplied output tokens by between 9 and 925 times, with the premium itself depending on the domain, and in most of them it lowered
run-to-run consistency, so the same answer scored itself less alike across repetitions. That consistency
loss is only partly mechanical: longer outputs give a non-deterministic backend more room to diverge, but
the link between an answer's length and its grade variability was moderate and uneven, so part of the
loss behaves like a property of reasoning itself rather than of length alone. The largest gains, about
0.25 QWK on RIAYN for Qwen3.5 and GPT-5.1, came where the model with reasoning off gave too many zeros,
while most other combinations gained little or nothing and a few lost ground. Such a small return rarely
justified a cost this large. The one dependable exception was a grader that collapsed to scoring much of
the code at exactly zero; there, reasoning restored the discrimination the grader had lost. This is why the advice about
reasoning is keyed to an observable behaviour, whether the baseline collapses, rather than to a model's
name, a choice that also lets it outlast the next model release.

Evaluation guidance was the cheapest reliable improvement we found. It raised agreement in 11 of 12
model-dataset combinations across three model families, by 0.10 to 0.26 QWK, and the benefit held on the
deployment data as on the public data. Providing it is the one configuration change in the study that
reliably improved agreement, and the only one whose benefit held for nearly every model and dataset.

How the grade was requested mattered far less. Grading a whole exam in one pass rather than question by
question, and scoring a rubric criterion by criterion rather than holistically, did not improve agreement
with the final grade, and for one model each they made it worse. Neither saved money either: grading
the whole exam at once cost more per question, not less, and scoring criterion by criterion doubled the
cost. The simple shape, one question per call with one holistic score, is therefore the default. A whole
exam here is about four questions; we did not test larger sets. The way the grading session is run had a
small effect of a different kind. Grading every answer inside one conversation that accumulated across
the exam pulled grades slightly down relative to giving each answer a clean conversation, and the order
in which answers were presented moved individual grades by about a tenth of the scale, in a direction
that depended on the model. Clean, question-by-question grading avoids both, which recommends it on
fairness grounds.

Among the models and configurations we tested, none graded best everywhere. On short answers the
configurations clustered within 0.04 QWK of one another, so the choice costs little agreement; on code
they spread far more widely, about 0.26 QWK, and there the configuration is worth optimising. Across
both tasks an open model with reasoning off was the efficient choice, GLM-5.1 most of all, because it
did not collapse on code and stayed near the top on short answers; the closed frontier model did not
repay its higher cost on the public data. The ranking learned on the public datasets carried over at the top in both
domains, and on code it carried over as a whole; on short answers, where the public data barely separate
the configurations, the order below the top did not. A configuration chosen on public data is therefore
a sound start, to be confirmed locally where the public ranking is close.

Taken together, these results argue for a structured set of conditional choices rather than a single
recommended grader. The deciding factors recurred throughout the study: the task, the model's observable
behaviour, the reliability of the reference, and the quality being optimised. Two of the three quantities
that matter, consistency and cost, are routinely left unreported in other studies, yet both moved against
reasoning here, so reporting agreement alone would have hidden half of what a practitioner needs to
decide. Because the
choices turn on observable behaviour rather than on model identity, they also do not expire with each new
release. We therefore express the contribution as a configuration guide: a small set
of task-keyed priors, each carrying its own trade-off. The next section presents it.
