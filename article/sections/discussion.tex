\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Read together, the five research questions and the follow-up experiment point to one practical message:
what the grader is given, and how it is told to use it, mattered more than how long it thinks or how much
it costs. The two cheap changes, supplying
a rubric or a reference answer and asking for partial credit, raised agreement more reliably than any
expensive one. Turning reasoning on, paying for a closed model, grading a whole exam in one call, and
scoring a rubric criterion by criterion cost more or saved little, and none of them reliably brought the
grades closer to the teachers'.

Reasoning is the expensive setting that most often disappointed. It raised agreement in only five of twenty
model-dataset combinations, three after correction, while multiplying the output tokens by up to 925 and
making grades less consistent in most combinations. The consistency loss is only partly mechanical: longer
outputs give a non-deterministic backend more room to diverge, but length explained the variability only
moderately and unevenly, so part of the loss behaves like a property of reasoning itself. Consistency is
not the same as quality either: the most reproducible grader with reasoning off, Qwen3.5, was also the one
with the strongest zero-collapse.

The zero-collapse explains where reasoning did pay. Its large gains came on code, for the two models that
scored many partially correct answers as zero. Graders that are too severe have been described before:
the authors of RIAYN measure a grader's leniency against the experts~\cite{pathak_rubric_2025}, strict
and lenient prompts have been compared~\cite{venkatraman_llmbased_2026}, and in a concurrent study a
lenient prompt that awards partial credit lowered the question-level grading error of every model family on a
mathematics exam~\cite{sapkota_math_2026}. The zero-collapse is a specific, measurable form of that
severity, revealed by a share of zeros far above the teachers'. The written justifications show what goes wrong: the
collapsing model sees what the student did right and still gives no marks, as if any error voided the
answer. An instruction to award partial credit reduced the zeros of the closed model GPT-5.1, but not
those of Qwen3.5, while reasoning, which works through the rubric before scoring, roughly halved Qwen3.5's
zeros. The collapse is therefore a property of the model with
reasoning off, which the instruction can soften but not always remove, and the dependable remedy is to
grade with a model that does not show it. Model size may also play a part. Four of the five significant
reasoning gains belong to Qwen3.5-35B-A3B, which activates only about 3B parameters per token, and the
gains of thinking reported for LLM judges were measured on Qwen3 models of 0.6B to
4B parameters~\cite{jayarao_explicit_2025}. With a single small model in the study, the effect of its size
cannot be separated from that of its zero-collapse.

Evaluation guidance and the grading instruction are the opposite case: cheap and effective. Guidance raised
agreement significantly in 13 of 15 combinations, by up to 0.341 QWK; on short answers it added more than the whole 0.11 spread between the eight model and reasoning
configurations in eight of the nine combinations. Asking for partial credit then added up to 0.11 on code,
for a few extra input tokens, and changed nothing on short answers. The
simplest shape of the request also won: one question per call with a single holistic score was never beaten
by the whole exam, and criterion scoring beat it only for GPT-5.1 on RIAYN, where it made a model that gives
too many zeros credit the rubric step by step; neither alternative saved more than 11\% of the cost. A clean
conversation per answer
removes the small pull of a shared history and the effect of an answer's position, so it is also the fairer
choice: a student's grade should not depend on where an answer fell in a session.

Model choice mattered more on code than on short answers: on code the configurations spread about twice
as widely, largely according to whether a model showed the zero-collapse. Asking for partial credit also changes which model is worth paying for: with it, the
cheapest model, DeepSeek-V4-Flash, graded code as well as GLM-5.1, the strongest grader without reasoning,
at about a tenth of its cost. The closed models bought little for their price: GPT-5.1 with reasoning,
the best code configuration, cost 34 times as much as GLM-5.1 without it for 0.031 more, and Claude Sonnet
4.6, the best on the science and PT-CS short answers, 43 to 53 times as much as the guide with
DeepSeek-V4-Flash. Absolute levels should be read against the
agreement between humans: on Mohler the models' agreement with each teacher (0.39 to 0.75) brackets the two
teachers' agreement with each other (0.49), so the remaining disagreement between model and teacher is of the
size that separates two human graders.

The PT-CS answers, graded by teachers in Portuguese, reproduced the effects found on the public datasets:
guidance helped as much, the same models scored too many code answers as zero, and partial credit raised
the agreement of DeepSeek-V4-Flash and GPT-5.1 on PT-CS code as it did on RIAYN. Taken together, the results support one configuration built from
the cheap settings, which the next section presents, tests, and validates.
