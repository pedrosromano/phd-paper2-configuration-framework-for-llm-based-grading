\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Read together, the six questions point to one practical message: what the grader is given, and how it is
told to use it, mattered more than how long it thinks or how much it costs. The two cheap changes, supplying
a rubric or a reference answer and asking for partial credit, raised agreement more reliably than any
expensive one. Turning reasoning on, paying for a closed model, grading a whole exam in one call, and
scoring a rubric criterion by criterion cost more or saved little, and none of them reliably brought the
grades closer to the teachers'. The paragraphs below interpret this message and what it implies for
configuring a grader.

Reasoning is the expensive setting that most often disappointed. It raised agreement in only six of twenty
model-dataset combinations, three after correction, while multiplying the output tokens by up to 925 and
making grades less consistent in most combinations. The consistency loss is only partly mechanical: longer
outputs give a non-deterministic backend more room to diverge, but length explained the variability only
moderately and unevenly (Section~\ref{sec:rq1}), so part of the loss behaves like a property of reasoning
itself. Consistency is not the same as quality either: the most reproducible grader with reasoning off,
Qwen3.5, was also the one that showed the zero-collapse.

The zero-collapse explains where reasoning did pay. Its large gains came on code, for the two models that
scored many partially correct answers as zero. The written justifications show what goes wrong: the
collapsing model sees what the student did right and still gives no marks, as if any error voided the
answer. An instruction to award partial credit did not change that, while reasoning, which works through the
rubric before scoring, roughly halved the zeros (Sections~\ref{sec:rq1} and~\ref{sec:rq6}). The collapse is
therefore a property of the model with reasoning off, and the cheaper remedy is a model that grades soundly
without it.

Evaluation guidance and the grading instruction are the opposite case: cheap and effective. Guidance raised
agreement significantly in 13 of 15 model-dataset combinations across three model families, by 0.10 to 0.34
QWK; on the public short answers the reference answer added more than the whole spread between the eight
model and reasoning configurations on the same task. Asking for partial credit then added up to 0.07 on code
for a model that grades soundly, for a few extra input tokens, and changed nothing on short answers. The
simplest shape of the request also won: one question per call with a single holistic score was never beaten
by the whole exam or by criterion scoring, which cost as much or more. A clean conversation per answer
removes the small pull of a shared history and the effect of an answer's position, so it is also the fairer
choice: a student's grade should not depend on where an answer fell in a session.

Model choice mattered far more on code than on short answers. On short answers the configurations lay
close together, while on code they spread widely, largely according to whether a model collapsed. The two
closed models did not repay their price on code; Claude Sonnet 4.6 graded the science and PT-CS short
answers best, at 43 to 53 times the cost of the recipe's model. Absolute levels should be read against the
agreement between humans: on Mohler the models' agreement with each teacher (0.39 to 0.75) brackets the two
teachers' agreement with each other (0.49), so the remaining disagreement between model and teacher is of the
size that separates two human graders.

The PT-CS answers, graded by teachers in Portuguese, reproduced the effects found on the public datasets:
guidance helped as much, the same models scored too many code answers as zero, and partial credit raised the
sound model's agreement on PT-CS code as it did on RIAYN. Taken together, the results support one recipe built from
the cheap settings, which the next section presents and tests.
