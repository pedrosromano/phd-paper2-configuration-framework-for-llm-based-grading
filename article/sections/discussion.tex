\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Read together, the six questions point to one practical message: the expensive settings rarely paid,
and the cheapest change almost always did. Turning reasoning on, paying for the closed frontier model,
grading a whole exam in one call, and scoring a rubric criterion by criterion cost more or saved little,
and none of them reliably brought the grades closer to the teachers'. Supplying a rubric or a reference
answer cost 1.2 to 1.8 times as much per grade and raised agreement in nearly every case. The paragraphs
below interpret each part of this message and what it implies for choosing a configuration.

Reasoning is the expensive setting that most often disappointed. It improved agreement significantly in
five of twenty model-dataset combinations, two after correcting for multiple comparisons, while
multiplying output tokens by 9 to 925 times and making grades less consistent across repetitions in most
combinations. The consistency loss is only partly mechanical: longer outputs give a non-deterministic
backend more room to diverge, but length explained the variability only moderately and unevenly
(Section~\ref{sec:rq1}), so part of the loss behaves like a property of reasoning itself. Consistency is
not the same as quality either: the most reproducible grader with reasoning off, Qwen3.5, was also the one
that collapsed. The largest reasoning gains, 0.23 to 0.25 QWK on RIAYN for GPT-5.1 and Qwen3.5, came for
the two models that, with reasoning off, scored too many partially correct answers as zero. The zero rate
is therefore the most useful diagnostic the study produced: measured with reasoning off on a set of
teacher-graded answers, it picked out both the weakest code graders and the only ones that reasoning
helped. The guide ties its code advice to that behaviour, which anyone can check, rather than to a model's
name.

Evaluation guidance is the opposite case, the cheapest change and the most reliable one. It raised
agreement significantly in 13 of 15 model-dataset combinations across three model families, by 0.10 to
0.34 QWK, and by as much on the deployment data as on the public data. On the public short answers the
reference answer added 0.095 to 0.256, more than the whole spread between the eight model and reasoning
configurations on the same task (0.074), and at a small fraction of what reasoning cost.

The shape of the request mattered less, and the simple shape won. Grading a whole exam in one call and
scoring criterion by criterion brought no significant gain, each made agreement significantly worse for
one model, and neither saved much: the whole exam changed the cost per question by $-6$ to $+7$\%, and
criterion scoring cost 1.3 to 3.1 times as much. More structure and more context in a single call did not
make the grader more accurate. The grading session had a small effect of a different kind: a shared
conversation pulled grades slightly down, and answer order moved individual grades by 8 to 10\% of the
question's marks, in a direction that depended on the model. A student's grade should not depend on where
an answer fell in a session, so grading each answer in a clean conversation is also the fairer choice.

Model choice mattered far more on code than on short answers. On short answers the eight configurations
fell within 0.074 QWK of one another; on code they spread over 0.259, largely according to whether a model
collapsed to zero. GLM-5.1 with reasoning off was the best of the four models on both tasks, and the closed
frontier model did not repay its higher cost on the public data. Absolute levels should be read against
the agreement between humans: on Mohler the models' agreement with each teacher (0.39 to 0.75) brackets
the two teachers' agreement with each other (0.49), so the remaining disagreement between model and
teacher is of the size that separates two human graders. In the deployment, the effects held while the
fine ranking did not: guidance helped as much, the collapsing model again graded code worst, and GLM-5.1
again led code grading, but configurations a few hundredths of QWK apart changed places. A configuration
chosen on public data is therefore a sound start, and the choice between close configurations belongs to
a local check.

Taken together, the results support a short default with a few checked exceptions. Consistency and cost
both moved against reasoning, so a comparison on agreement alone would have hidden half of what a
practitioner needs to decide. Because the exceptions turn on behaviour a user can check, such as whether a
model collapses to zero, the same checks can be run on models we did not test. The next section turns
these findings into the configuration guide.
