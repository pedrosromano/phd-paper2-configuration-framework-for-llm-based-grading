\begin{abstract}
Instructors adopting graders based on large language models (LLMs) must answer several configuration
questions. Should the model reason before scoring? What guidance should it receive? Should an exam be
graded in one call or question by question? Which configuration is worth its cost? We study these
choices for computer science assessment, varying extended reasoning, evaluation guidance,
grading scope, rubric decomposition, model family, and conversation state under one protocol, on four
datasets covering short answers and code. Reasoning is switched on and off within each of four model
families, three open-weight and one closed, and every measurement is repeated, so cost and run-to-run
consistency are measured alongside quality. Reasoning raised agreement with teacher grades
in at most five of twenty model-dataset combinations, two after correcting for multiple comparisons, at
9 to 925 times the output tokens, and it reduced consistency in seventeen of the twenty. Evaluation
guidance raised quadratic weighted kappa (QWK) significantly in thirteen of fifteen combinations across
three model families, by 0.10 to 0.34. Grading a whole exam in one call and scoring criterion by criterion
brought no significant gain. Configuration rankings on public code data carried over to a real deployment; on
short answers, where configurations differed by at most 0.04 QWK, only the top did. We turn these comparisons into a configuration guide that maps a grading task to a
recommended configuration and its cost; its default stayed within 0.08 QWK of the best configuration on
every dataset, at up to 30 times lower cost.
\end{abstract}
